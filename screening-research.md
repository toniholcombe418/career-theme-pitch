# How Resumes and LinkedIn Profiles Get Screened

Read this before writing summaries (Step 8) or rewriting a LinkedIn profile (Step 9). Each finding notes how strong the source is, so you can weigh it.

## 1. Human recruiters skim first, then read

**Source: Ladders eye-tracking studies, 2012 and 2018. Moderate strength.**

- The 2012 study tracked about 30 recruiters. The average first screen lasted about 6 seconds.
- The 2018 update found 7.4 seconds. The report does not state sample size, role types, or resume lengths, so treat the exact number loosely.
- The first screen is a gate, not the full read. Resumes that pass it get a much longer look.
- Recruiters scan job titles first.
- Resumes with simple layouts, clear sections, and bold titles held attention. Cluttered layouts, multiple columns, long sentences, and missing headers did poorly.
- The studies covered recruiters, not hiring managers. The user's audience is both.

**What this means:** Make titles, the first bullet under the latest job, and the summary easy to find and fast to read.

## 2. The summary section is a weak spot

**Source: career blogs citing eye-tracking work. Weak strength, sources unclear.**

- Several blogs claim summaries get 2 to 3 seconds, or that dense 3 to 4 sentence summary paragraphs get skipped. No solid primary study confirms this.
- One recruiter's list of what they check first did not include the summary at all: latest title, company name, then the first line under the latest job.

**What this means:** Keep the summary to three short lines. Do not rely on it alone. The first bullet under the latest job may carry more weight.

## 3. Recruiters look for specific things

**Source: recruiters and HR leaders posting on Reddit. Anecdotal, but consistent with each other and with Ladders.**

- They need to find what the person did, how they did it, and the result.
- One recruiter wants about 75% of the listed qualifications on the resume before a phone screen.
- Hard requirements, such as a required degree, end the read if missing.
- The first verb of each bullet decides whether the rest gets read.
- An opening phrase that states an impressive, relevant outcome gets the whole bullet read.
- Skip triggers: "passionate, results-driven professional," "responsible for" lines, and a long paragraph at the top.
- Keep-reading triggers: one plain line on what the person fixed, built, or improved, and something specific, even without a big number.
- Titles without scope force guessing. "Program Manager" could mean a small project or a $20M program.
- The frame: early review is about reducing doubt. "Is there enough here to safely move this person forward?"
- Quick test: read the first line out loud. If it sounds like something you would tell a friend, keep it. If it sounds like a LinkedIn bio, rewrite it.

## 4. Software reads before people do

**Source: Harvard Business School "Hidden Workers" report. Strong.**

- 75% of 760 U.S. employers surveyed used automated tools in hiring.

**What this means:** The summary and skills must use the posting's words, and the format must be simple enough for software to parse: one column, standard headings, no tables or graphics.

## 5. LinkedIn's AI hiring tools

**Source: LinkedIn's Hiring Assistant marketing pages, demo images, AI transparency page, and data flow diagram. Official, but demo images may not match real behavior.**

How it works:

- A supervisor agent runs four sub-agents: Intake, Sourcing, Evaluation, and Outreach.
- **Intake** turns the recruiter's job description into "hiring intent": job title variants, locations, required qualifications, and nice-to-have qualifications. In one demo, a qualified profile had to meet 80% of required and 40% of nice-to-have qualifications.
- **Sourcing** plans searches, then runs LinkedIn Recruiter Search. Candidates are found through their profiles before they ever apply.
- **Evaluation** compares each candidate to hiring intent. It draws on four sources, as available: the LinkedIn profile, a public resume, answers to screening questions, and recruiter notes.
- It sorts applicants into Top, Maybe a fit, and Not a fit.
- It writes its own short summary of each candidate. The recruiter may read that before the candidate's own summary.
- It quotes resume and profile lines word for word as evidence for each qualification.
- It lists gaps plainly, such as "Missing experience in big tech company."
- It shows a skills match count, such as 5 of 10 skills matching the posting, and a keyword match level.
- It reruns when applications arrive, when candidates change stages, when recruiters give feedback, and on a schedule. Profile updates can change a score over time.
- AI-assisted search can match soft qualities not listed as skills, such as "data-driven decision maker." LinkedIn does not say what evidence it needs.
- In LinkedIn Recruiter search results, recruiters see the headline, current and past titles, companies, dates, and education. The About section is not shown at that stage.

**What this means:**

- The headline and titles decide whether a profile surfaces at all.
- Each experience line should stand alone as proof of one qualification, because the AI quotes single lines.
- Stories prove soft qualities. Adjectives only claim them.
- Skills should match the words target postings use.
- Screening question answers get scored too. Write them like strong bullets.
- Keep the profile current, since scores are recalculated.

## 6. What the research does not cover

- No solid study tests a problem-first summary against a standard one. Treat the problem-first approach as a strong idea to test, not a proven rule.
- One real-world signal: a CFO told a candidate that her opening line, "Paralegal turned data scientist," caught his attention, and he connected it to her problem solving on his own. That is one reader, so treat contrast lines as worth testing too.
