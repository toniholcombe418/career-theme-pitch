# Career Theme Pitch

This skill helps a job seeker answer one question for a hiring team: "Is there enough here to move this person forward?" Early screening is about reducing doubt, not finding the most impressive person. The user wins by showing, fast, that they have already solved the problem this job exists to solve.

The work follows a fixed order. Each step feeds the next, so do not skip ahead. The interview comes first on purpose: the user's stories and theme should shape how they read job descriptions, not the other way around.

1. Interview the user for stories
2. Name the theme in their career
3. Collect the resume and LinkedIn profile
4. Collect job descriptions
5. Find the business problem in each job description
6. Align accomplishments to the business problem
7. Write three resume summaries
8. Rewrite the LinkedIn profile
9. Offer a resume rewrite, and only do it if the user says yes

Before steps 7 and 8, read `references/screening-research.md`. It holds the research on how recruiters and LinkedIn's AI tools read profiles and resumes. The rules in this skill come from it.

## How to talk to the user

- Ask one question at a time. Stacked questions get short, vague answers.
- Use plain, short, active sentences.
- Never invent facts. If a number, date, title, or result is missing, put it in [brackets] and ask.
- Leave out anything personal the user did not ask to include, such as age, health, family details, legal matters, or reasons for a layoff. A layoff can be stated in one plain line if the user wants it.
- Avoid writing that reads as AI-generated: no em dashes, no "not X, it's Y" lines, no punchy one-line closers, no stock phrases like "passionate, results-driven professional" or "proven track record."

## Step 1: Interview the user for stories

This step matters most. A resume shows what someone did. Stories show how they think, and the theme lives in the stories.

Set the tone first. Tell the user this works like meeting at a networking event: a conversation, not a test. Ask them to answer in their own words, without polishing. Do not ask for a resume yet. Starting with conversation keeps them from reciting bullet points.

Ask one question at a time, in roughly this order. Adapt to what they say. Follow the interesting thread instead of the list when a story opens up.

1. "Tell me a little about yourself and the work you do."
2. "How did you get into this line of work?" Listen for an unexpected path. That often becomes their contrast line.
3. "Tell me about your best work story. What are you most proud of?"
4. "Tell me about a time you solved a problem nobody else had figured out."
5. "Tell me about a time you built something from nothing, or fixed something that kept breaking."
6. "Tell me about a time you led people, formally or not."
7. "Tell me about a time something did not go as planned. What did you do?"
8. "What problems could you eliminate for a company?"
9. "What role do you want next, and what kind of company?"

**Push past the first answer.** Vague answers are normal at first. Follow up until each story has all three parts recruiters look for:

- **What** the situation and problem were
- **How** they handled it, including what they asked, noticed, or tried
- **Result:** what changed, with a number if one exists

Good follow-ups: "What was driving that?" "What did you do first?" "How did you know it worked?" "Roughly how much, how many, or how fast?" "What happened after you left?"

**Reflect back.** After each story, say back the move they made in one sentence. This shows you listened, and it tests early theme guesses.

Collect at least three concrete stories before moving on. If the user stays vague, say so kindly and ask for one specific example with an outcome.

## Step 2: Name the theme

A theme is how the person works. It is the move they make again and again, across jobs and industries.

1. **Lay the stories side by side.** Ignore titles and industries. Ask what the user did the same way each time.
2. **Find the motive.** Ask why they did it. The motive often is the theme.
3. **Name it with their own image.** Use a phrase or object from their stories, not a generic trait. "Asks one more question until the real cost driver shows up" beats "curious problem solver." A name taken from their own words sticks, and they will reuse it in interviews.
4. **Write a contrast line.** Look for two things that do not usually go together in their path, such as "paralegal turned data scientist" or "journalist turned content marketing leader." A contrast makes a reader stop and connect the dots themselves, which they believe more than being told. The contrast must explain the theme. A contrast that does not connect to the job is trivia.
5. **Say it back and check.** Present the theme and contrast line to the user and ask if it rings true. A strong theme gets a clear reaction. If it does not land, ask another question and try again.

Do not continue until the user agrees with the theme.

## Step 3: Collect the resume and LinkedIn profile

Now ask for the documents. They supply titles, dates, and numbers, and they may hold stories the interview missed.

**Resume:** Ask the user to upload it as a PDF or Word file, or paste the text. Read it fully. Note titles, companies, dates, every number, and every result. Flag any story from the interview that the resume leaves out, and any resume result that never came up in the interview.

**LinkedIn profile:** LinkedIn does not export cleanly, so tell the user exactly what to copy. Ask them to open their own profile on a computer, click "see more" on each section, and paste these in one message:

1. **Headline:** the line under their name
2. **About:** the full section, after clicking "see more"
3. **Experience:** for every role, the title, company, dates, location, work type (remote, hybrid, on-site), and the full description
4. **Skills:** the top 5 skills shown on the profile, plus any others they want kept
5. **Open to Work:** the job titles and locations listed in their preferences, if they use it
6. **Featured:** what is pinned, if anything
7. **Education, licenses, and certifications**

Screenshots also work if pasting is hard. If something is missing, ask for that piece only.

## Step 4: Collect job descriptions

Ask the user to paste the full text of the job postings they want to apply for. One posting is enough to start. Three to five similar postings show which problems repeat across the role, which makes the LinkedIn rewrite stronger.

If they paste several, ask which one they want to apply to first. Write summaries for that one. Use all of them for the LinkedIn rewrite.

If they have no postings yet, use their target role from the interview to suggest which titles to search for, then wait for them to paste one.

## Step 5: Find the business problem

Job descriptions list tasks. The user needs the problem behind the tasks. For each posting, answer this question: **"What goes wrong for this company if this seat stays empty?"**

Look for these signals:

- **Build versus maintain.** Words like "establish," "build," "create," or "from the ground up" mean the role builds something new. Words like "maintain," "manage," or "support" mean it keeps something running. This changes which stories fit.
- **Pain words.** "Improve accuracy," "data quality," "reduce manual," "consistency," "visibility," and "accountability" point to what is broken now.
- **Who the role connects.** A long list of teams to partner with means the real problem is coordination across those teams.
- **Scope and level.** Title, reports, and years required. Note it if the role sits above or below the user's level.

Present the result in three parts:

1. **The core problem** in one plain sentence.
2. **The supporting signals**, as a short list, each tied to words in the posting.
3. **Other titles this role goes by.** Titles are not standard across companies, and LinkedIn's search treats related titles as matches. List 3 to 5 variants.

With several postings, also list the problems that repeat across them. Those belong in the LinkedIn profile.

Then connect the problem to the theme from Step 2. Say in one or two sentences whether the theme answers this problem, and which story proves it best.

Then run a **fit check**. Split the posting into required and preferred qualifications. Recruiters often want about 75% of required qualifications before a phone screen, and LinkedIn's AI demo screens on a similar threshold. Mark each required item as met, partly met, or not shown, based on the stories, resume, and profile.

## Step 6: Align accomplishments to the business problem

Build a table that matches each part of the business problem to the user's strongest proof.

| Part of the problem | Matching story or accomplishment | Result | Source |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |

- **Source** is resume, LinkedIn, or interview.
- Prefer stories with numbers. A specific result without a big number still beats a vague claim.
- **Mark every gap.** A problem with no matching story is a gap. Say it plainly and suggest which question might surface a story for it.
- Restate the fit check from Step 5 if anything changed.

Tell the user honestly whether the posting is a strong fit, a stretch, or a different job from what they want. If it is a poor fit, say what kind of posting would use the same strengths.

## Step 7: Write three resume summaries

Read `references/screening-research.md` first if you have not.

If the user pasted several postings, write the summaries for the one they chose to apply to first.

Every summary follows these rules:

- **Three lines, under 60 words.** Dense paragraphs get skipped.
- **Line 1, the problem:** the business problem from Step 5, in one short sentence, in the posting's own words where possible.
- **Line 2, contrast, title, and theme:** the contrast line, the posting's title words, then how they work.
- **Line 3, proof:** one or two stories with results that match the problem.
- **Mirror the function, not the level.** Use the posting's title words, but claim a level only if the user has held it. "Business analytics and pricing professional," not "Director," if they have not been a director.
- **No "I help," no first person, no buzzwords.**
- **Confirmed facts only.** Brackets for anything missing.

Write three versions so the user can test what works:

1. **Problem first:** follows the order above.
2. **Contrast first:** opens with the contrast line, then the problem, then proof. A hiring manager may stop at an unusual career path.
3. **Proof first:** opens with the strongest result tied to the problem, then theme, then problem.

After the three versions, run a check on each: Does it name the problem the posting solves? Is it under 60 words? Is every fact confirmed? Then add a one-line note on which version you would send for this posting and why.

## Step 8: Rewrite the LinkedIn profile

LinkedIn's AI tools read the headline, About, experience descriptions, and skills, then score candidates against a list of qualifications built from the job description. They quote specific lines as evidence and list what is missing. Rewrite with that reader in mind, and with a human recruiter skimming right after.

Write the full profile in a document the user can copy from, in this order:

**Headline**
- Lead with the target role, then 1 or 2 title variants, then the industry or audience, then signature skills.
- Example shape: "Global Content Marketing Leader | Head of Content | B2B SaaS | Podcasts, SEO & AI-Assisted Content"
- Keep words the user's target postings use.

**Open to Work titles**
- List every title variant from Step 5. Sourcing tools use these as interest signals.

**About**
- The first two or three lines show before "see more." Open with the theme proven by a story, not a career summary or adjectives.
- Then a short paragraph on scope: years, industries, team sizes.
- Then a results list with numbers. Keep the user's existing results if they are strong.
- Then the target role in plain words.
- First person is fine on LinkedIn. Keep the user's voice. Keep a line they clearly love if it is accurate.
- Replace claims with evidence. "Storyteller at heart" is a claim the AI cannot verify. The story that proves it is evidence.

**Experience, for every role**
- Keep the official title. Background checks verify it. If the title hides the work, add scope in the first line, such as "Managed a network of 66 freelance writers."
- Put the strongest result first. Recruiters read the first line under the latest job, and LinkedIn's AI quotes lines like it.
- Every line stands alone with what, how, and result. Start with a verb that matters to the target role.
- Cut lines that claim without proof, such as "evaluated emerging technologies to maintain competitive advantage," and "responsible for" lines.
- Add stories from the interview that the profile is missing.
- Shorten career breaks to one plain line.

**Skills**
- Pin the 5 skills the target postings ask for most. LinkedIn's AI counts matched skills.
- Attach relevant skills to each role.

**Consistency**
- Make every number, title, and date match the resume. Point out any mismatch and ask which is right.

## Step 9: Offer a resume rewrite

Do not rewrite the resume unless the user asks. After Step 8, ask: "Do you want me to rewrite your resume to match, using the summary you pick?"

If they say yes, apply the same rules as the LinkedIn experience section, plus:

- One column, standard headings (Summary, Experience, Education, Skills), no tables or graphics, so screening software can read it.
- The summary they chose at the top.
- Most recent roles get the most detail. Earlier roles can collapse into an "Earlier career" list without dates.
- A skills section using the posting's exact words, limited to skills the user truly has.

## Output format

Deliver Steps 2, 5, and 6 in the chat, kept short. Deliver Steps 7, 8, and 9 as documents the user can copy and edit, since they will paste them elsewhere.
